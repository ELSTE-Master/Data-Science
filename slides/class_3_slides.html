How does it work?

  • The data are the outcome of a random process: they form a random sample of a (possibly conceptual) population.
  • Statistical inference uses the sample to learn about the population: estimating quantities and testing hypotheses.
  • Examples of research questions:
    • pharma: does a new drug lower blood pressure more than the standard of care?
    • earth: do magmas erupting through thick crust have a higher SiO\(_2\) content than those erupting through thin crust?

Source: luminousmen

The big picture: p-hacking

P-hacking is the misuse of data analysis to find patterns that can be presented as statistically significant: run many tests on the data and report only those that come back significant. Example: the number of letters in the Scripps National Spelling Bee winning word correlates with the number of people killed by venomous spiders in the US.

Source: Wikipedia on data dredging. 👋 If you want to know more, take a look here.

The garden of forking paths

  • No fishing needed: every analysis involves choices made after seeing the data (which groups, which test, one- or two-sided, what to do with outliers, …). Each path looks reasonable, and only one is reported.
  • The data of this class, 16 defensible analyses: p-values from \(1.8\%\) to \(86\%\), six of them below \(5\%\). The reported p-value ignores the paths not taken.
  • Remedies: pre-register the path before looking at the data, or report the whole garden (multiverse analysis).

This brings us to “HARKing”

HARKing is an acronym coined by Norbert Kerr for the questionable research practice of “Hypothesizing After the Results are Known”: presenting a post hoc hypothesis in the introduction of a research report as if it were an a priori hypothesis.

The costs of HARKing (and p-hacking)

HARKing and p-hacking are (arguably) playing an important role in the replication crisis in science: when 100 published psychology studies were repeated, most p-values and effect sizes did not hold up.

Source: Open Science Collaboration, “Estimating the reproducibility of psychological science”, Science 349 (2015). 👋 If you want to know more, take a look here.

The cost for society

Contradictory studies (coffee, wine, milk, …) make the public cynical about science: “find the study that sounds best to you and go with that”. Once evidence can be picked like that, it has lost its power.

Case study

Hypothesis: thicker crust above subduction zones should produce lavas with higher silica (SiO\(_2\)) content.

👋 Farner, M.J. & Lee, C.-T.A. (2017). Earth and Planetary Science Letters, 470, 96–107. doi:10.1016/j.epsl.2017.04.025

Case study — why would it be so?

  • Under thick crust, rising magmas travel further and are stored longer at depth: they cool, crystallize (and assimilate crust) more, so the erupted magma is more differentiated, i.e. richer in SiO\(_2\).
  • Scientific hypothesis: magmas erupting through thick crust have a higher SiO\(_2\) content than those erupting through thin crust.
  • Question for this class: is this visible in the data, and how sure can we be of what we see?

Case study — a global study

  • Compare magma geochemistry of samples from multiple locations: 26 arcs, 8498 cells of \(10 \times 10\) km (colours: crustal-thickness class, next slide).
  • Data sourced from GEOROC, the public online geochemistry database.

Case study — defining the groups

  • Sample elevation used as a proxy for crustal thickness.
  • Continuous variable (elevation, m a.s.l.) broken into discrete groups.
  • Major-element concentrations can be compared between each group to test for statistically significant differences.
  • E.g. SiO\(_2\), MgO, FeO, CaO.
  • In this class: three crustal-thickness groups, thin \(< 30\) km, medium \(30\)–\(43\) km, thick \(\geq 43\) km, and SiO\(_2\).

Hypothesis testing

  • A scientific hypothesis is translated into two mutually exclusive statistical hypotheses:

\[H_0: \theta\hspace{0.1cm} {\color{#eb078e}{\in}}\hspace{0.1cm}\Theta_0 \quad \text{and} \quad H_a: \theta\hspace{0.1cm} {\color{#eb078e}{\not\in}}\hspace{0.1cm}\Theta_0\]

  • Null hypothesis \(\color{#373895}{H_0}\): the statement being tested; the test weighs the evidence against it.
  • Alternative hypothesis \(\color{#373895}{H_a}\): what we suspect (or hope) to be true instead.

Hypothesis testing

Standard procedure

  1. Hypotheses: state \(H_0\) and \(H_a\) from the research question.
  2. Significance level: choose \(\alpha\), the probability of rejecting \(H_0\) when it is true (typically \(5\%\)), before looking at the results.
  3. p-value: choose a suitable test (look at the data) and compute its p-value, the probability, if \(H_0\) were true, of a result at least as extreme as the one observed (⚠️ it is not the probability that \(H_0\) is true).
  4. Decision:
    • \(\text{p-value} < \alpha\): reject \(H_0\) and accept \(H_a\), the result is significant.
    • \(\text{p-value} \geq \alpha\): do not reject \(H_0\), nothing can be concluded (“absence of evidence is not evidence of absence”).


Decision \(H_0\) is true \(H_0\) is false
Don’t reject \(H_0\) ✅ correct (prob \(1-\alpha\)) ⚠️ Type II error (prob \(\beta\))
Reject \(H_0\) ⚠️ Type I error (prob \(\alpha\)) ✅ correct (prob \(1-\beta\), the power)

Two-sample location tests

Goal: compare the means (or locations) of two independent samples. For example:

  1. A volcanologist: mean SiO\(_2\) content of magmas erupting through thin vs medium crust.
  2. An ecologist: weight of salamanders in an old-growth forest vs a clear-cut area.
  3. A limnologist: duration of lake ice cover before vs after 1950.
  4. Does coffee raise blood pressure? ☕
  5. Does the US economy grow faster under Democratic or Republican presidents? 🇺🇸 (see the beginning of the class)
  6. Is a glass of red wine as good as an hour at the gym? 🍷

We will see three tests:

  1. Student’s t-test: assumes equal variances.
  2. Welch’s t-test: no equal-variance assumption.
  3. Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon test: based on ranks, robust to outliers.

Student’s t-test

Model for groups A and B (\(g = A, B\) and \(i = 1, \ldots, n_g\)), with the same variance in both groups:

\[X_{i(g)} = {\color{#eb078e}{\mu_{g}}} + \varepsilon_{i(g)}, \qquad \varepsilon_{i(g)} \overset{iid}{\sim} N(0,{\color{#eb078e}{\sigma^{2}}}).\]

Hypotheses (usually \(\mu_0 = 0\)) and test statistic under \(H_0\) (\(s_p\) = pooled standard deviation):

\[H_0: {\color{#6A5ACD}{\mu_A}} - {\color{#06bcf1}{\mu_B}} {\color{#eb078e}{=}} \mu_0 \quad \text{vs} \quad H_a: {\color{#6A5ACD}{\mu_A}} - {\color{#06bcf1}{\mu_B}} \ \big[ {\color{#eb078e}{>}} \text{ or } {\color{#eb078e}{<}} \text{ or } {\color{#eb078e}{\neq}} \big]\ \mu_0, \qquad T = \frac{(\overline{X}_{A}-\overline{X}_{B})-\mu_0}{s_{p}\sqrt{n_{A}^{-1}+n_{B}^{-1}}} \ {\overset{H_0}{\sim}} \ \text{Student}(n_{A}+n_{B}-2).\]

from scipy import stats
stats.ttest_ind(a = ..., b = ..., alternative = ..., equal_var = True)
  • Needs approximately normal data without outliers (for small or moderate \(n\)).
  • The equal-variance assumption is hard to verify: we recommend avoiding this test in practice.

Welch’s t-test

Same model and hypotheses as Student’s test, but each group has its own variance: \(\varepsilon_{i(g)} \overset{iid}{\sim} N(0,{\color{#eb078e}{\sigma_g^{2}}})\).

Test statistic under \(H_0\) (\(df\) is computed from the data; the details are beyond the scope of this class):

\[T = \frac{(\overline{X}_{A}-\overline{X}_{B})-\mu_0}{\sqrt{s^2_A/n_{A} + s^2_B/n_{B}}} \ {\overset{H_0}{\sim}} \ \text{Student}(df).\]

from scipy import stats
stats.ttest_ind(a = ..., b = ..., alternative = ..., equal_var = False)
  • Needs approximately normal data without outliers (for small or moderate \(n\)).
  • Does not require equal variances. If they happen to be equal it loses only a little power compared to Student’s test: our default choice.

What if one value were wrong?

The smallest medium-crust value is an Andean basalt with SiO\(_2 = 46.0\) wt.%. Suppose it had been entered incorrectly: let us give it larger and larger values and redo Welch’s t-test. (Yes, a percentage cannot exceed 100: this is a thought experiment, a typing error can produce any number.)

  • At first the p-value decreases: the medium mean moves away from the thin mean.
  • Then the inflated standard deviation makes the test lose significance: \(5.3\%\) at 200, \(16\%\) at 459.6.
  • One wrong value can flip the conclusion: we need a test robust to such values.

Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon test

Model for groups A and B, without normality: \(X_{i(g)} = {\color{#eb078e}{\theta_{g}}} + \varepsilon_{i(g)}\), \(\varepsilon_{i(g)} \overset{iid}{\sim} (0,\sigma^{2})\), where \(\theta_g\) is the population location of group \(g\).

\[H_0: {\color{#6A5ACD}{\theta_A}} - {\color{#06bcf1}{\theta_B}} {\color{#eb078e}{=}} \theta_0 \quad \text{vs} \quad H_a: {\color{#6A5ACD}{\theta_A}} - {\color{#06bcf1}{\theta_B}} \ \big[ {\color{#eb078e}{>}} \text{ or } {\color{#eb078e}{<}} \text{ or } {\color{#eb078e}{\neq}} \big]\ \theta_0.\]

The test statistic uses the ranks of the observations in the pooled sample, not their values (\(R_{i(g)}\) = global rank of the \(i\)-th observation of group \(g\)):

\[Z = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{B}}R_{i(B)}-n_{B}(n_{A}+n_{B}+1)/2}{\sqrt{n_{A}n_{B}(n_{A}+n_{B}+1)/12}}.\]

from scipy import stats
stats.mannwhitneyu(x = ..., y = ..., alternative = ...)
  • Robust: an extreme value only changes its rank, however large it is.
  • To compare means (\(\theta_g = \mu_g\)) the distributions should be symmetric with equal variances; otherwise it compares locations (medians). Slightly less powerful than the \(t\)-tests when their assumptions hold.

Thin vs medium crust — Wilcoxon

Same conclusion as Welch’s t-test: \(\text{p-value} = 4.75\% < 5\%\), we reject \(H_0\).

Robustness of the Wilcoxon test

Same experiment as before: the smallest medium-crust value is replaced by larger and larger values.

  • Once the value is the largest of its group its rank no longer changes: the Wilcoxon p-value stays at \(2.2\%\), whatever the value (even 459.6).
  • The \(t\)-test p-value crosses \(5\%\) around 200 and reaches \(16\%\) for the decimal slip.
  • Look at your data first: outliers or strong skewness are a good reason to prefer the Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon test.

Problems with multiple samples

Often we need to compare the means of more than 2 groups: for example the mean SiO\(_2\) content of magmas erupting through thin, medium and thick crust. Following Farner & Lee (2017), our theory is that differentiation increases with crustal thickness:

\(\color{#6A5ACD}{\mu_{\text{thin}}} < \color{#06bcf1}{\mu_{\text{medium}}} < \color{#8bb174}{\mu_{\text{thick}}}\)

A possible approach: three one-sided tests, hoping to reject \(H_0\) each time.

  1. \(H_0: \color{#6A5ACD}{\mu_{\text{thin}}} = \color{#06bcf1}{\mu_{\text{medium}}}\) vs \(H_a: \color{#6A5ACD}{\mu_{\text{thin}}} < \color{#06bcf1}{\mu_{\text{medium}}}\)
  2. \(H_0: \color{#06bcf1}{\mu_{\text{medium}}} = \color{#8bb174}{\mu_{\text{thick}}}\) vs \(H_a: \color{#06bcf1}{\mu_{\text{medium}}} < \color{#8bb174}{\mu_{\text{thick}}}\)
  3. \(H_0: \color{#6A5ACD}{\mu_{\text{thin}}} = \color{#8bb174}{\mu_{\text{thick}}}\) vs \(H_a: \color{#6A5ACD}{\mu_{\text{thin}}} < \color{#8bb174}{\mu_{\text{thick}}}\)

Multiple testing can be dangerous!

  • A p-value is random: it depends on the data.
  • One test at \(\alpha = 5\%\): a \(5\%\) chance of a false alarm. \(k\) tests: \(k\) chances.
  • For \(k\) independent tests, all \(H_0\) true:

\[\alpha_k = \Pr(\text{reject at least once}) = 1 - (1-\alpha)^k.\]

  • Jelly beans: \(k = 20\) colours, so \(\alpha_{20} = 1 - 0.95^{20} \approx 64\%\). The “discovery” was expected, not lucky.
  • ⚠️ Many tests, on the same or on different data, produce “significant” results even when nothing is there. Very common! 😟

Solution: a corrected level \(\alpha_c\)

Idea: we want the chance of at least one false rejection among the \(k\) tests, \(\alpha_k\), to be \(\alpha\) (say \(5\%\)). So we run each test at a smaller level \(\alpha_c\) instead of \(\alpha\).

Boole’s inequality: the probability of a union is at most the sum of the probabilities, whatever the dependence between the events:

\[\alpha_k = \Pr(\text{reject test 1 OR} \ \ldots \ \text{OR reject test } k) \ {\color{#eb078e}{\leq}} \ \sum_{i=1}^k \Pr(\text{reject test } i) = k \, \alpha_c.\]

Imposing \(k \, \alpha_c = \alpha\) gives the Bonferroni correction:

\[\color{#6A5ACD}{\alpha_c = \frac{\alpha}{k}}.\]

  • Always valid: no assumption on the dependence between the tests, nor on how many \(H_0\) are true.
  • Our three crust comparisons: \(\alpha_c = 5\%/3 \approx 1.67\%\). Jelly beans: \(5\%/20 = 0.25\%\).

Bonferroni, Dunn–Šidák and beyond

  • Bonferroni is conservative: \(\alpha_k\) ends up below \(\alpha\), so each test loses power, more and more as \(k\) grows.
  • For independent tests, \(\alpha_k\) is known exactly: \(\alpha_k = 1 - (1-\alpha_c)^k\). Setting it to \(\alpha\) gives the Dunn–Šidák correction:

\[\color{#6A5ACD}{\alpha_c = 1 - (1-\alpha)^{1/k}}.\]

\(k\) 2 3 10 20
Bonferroni \(2.50\%\) \(1.67\%\) \(0.50\%\) \(0.25\%\)
Dunn–Šidák \(2.53\%\) \(1.70\%\) \(0.51\%\) \(0.26\%\)

  • Slightly less stringent, but exact only for independent tests. Pairwise comparisons sharing a group are dependent: Bonferroni is the safe choice.
  • Thousands of tests (genomics): both corrections reject almost nothing. Methods controlling the False Discovery Rate are used instead (beyond this class).

Multiple-sample location tests

Goal: compare the means (or locations) of \(G \geq 2\) independent samples. For example, the mean SiO\(_2\) content of magmas erupting through thin, medium and thick crust.

Standard procedure in two steps:

  • Step 1: a multiple-sample location test of \(H_0\): “all \(G\) locations are equal”. If \(H_0\) is not rejected, we stop here.
  • Step 2: otherwise, we compare the groups pairwise with two-sample tests at a corrected level \(\alpha_c\) (Bonferroni or Dunn–Šidák) to check our scientific hypothesis.

We will see three multiple-sample location tests:

  1. Fisher’s one-way ANalysis Of VAriance (ANOVA): assumes equal variances.
  2. Welch’s one-way ANOVA: no equal-variance assumption.
  3. Kruskal-Wallis test: based on ranks, robust to outliers.

👋 Step 1 is a single test, whatever the number of groups: the multiple-testing problem (and \(\alpha_c\)) only appears at Step 2.

Fisher’s one-way ANOVA

Model for \(G\) groups (\(g = 1, \ldots, G\) and \(i = 1, \ldots, n_g\)), with the same variance in all groups:

\[X_{i(g)} = {\color{#eb078e}{\mu_{g}}} + \varepsilon_{i(g)}, \qquad \varepsilon_{i(g)} \overset{iid}{\sim} N(0,{\color{#eb078e}{\sigma^{2}}}).\]

Hypotheses:

\[H_0: {\color{#6A5ACD}{\mu_1}} {\color{#eb078e}{=}} {\color{#06bcf1}{\mu_2}} {\color{#eb078e}{=}} \ldots {\color{#eb078e}{=}} {\color{#8bb174}{\mu_G}} \quad \text{vs} \quad H_a: \mu_i {\color{#eb078e}{\neq}} \mu_j \ \text{ for at least one pair } (i,j).\]

Test statistic under \(H_0\): the variability between the group means divided by the variability within the groups, where \(N = \sum_g n_g\) and \(\overline{X}\) is the mean of all \(N\) observations. Means far apart compared to the noise give a large \(F\), i.e. a small p-value:

\[F = \frac{\frac{1}{G-1}\sum_{g=1}^G n_g \left(\overline{X}_g - \overline{X}\right)^2}{\frac{1}{N-G}\sum_{g=1}^G (n_g - 1)\, s_g^2} \ {\overset{H_0}{\sim}} \ \text{Fisher}(G-1,\, N-G).\]

from scipy import stats
stats.f_oneway(group_1, group_2, group_3)   # no "alternative": Ha is always "at least one pair differs"
  • Needs approximately normal data without outliers (for small or moderate \(n_g\)). For \(G = 2\) it is Student’s test (\(F = T^2\)).
  • The equal-variance assumption is hard to verify: we recommend avoiding this test in practice.

Welch’s one-way ANOVA

Same model and hypotheses as Fisher’s ANOVA, but each group has its own variance: \(\varepsilon_{i(g)} \overset{iid}{\sim} N(0,{\color{#eb078e}{\sigma_g^{2}}})\).

Test statistic under \(H_0\): each group mean is weighted by its precision \(w_g = n_g / s_g^2\) (the correction in the denominator and \(\Delta\) are computed from the data; the details are beyond the scope of this class):

\[F^* = \frac{\frac{1}{G-1}\sum_{g=1}^G w_g \left(\overline{X}_g - \overline{X}^*\right)^2}{1 + \frac{2(G-2)}{3\Delta}} \ {\overset{H_0}{\sim}} \ \text{Fisher}(G-1,\, \Delta), \qquad \overline{X}^* = \frac{\sum_{g=1}^G w_g \overline{X}_g}{\sum_{g=1}^G w_g}.\]

For the curious: \(\Delta = \Big[\frac{3}{G^2-1} \sum_{g=1}^G \frac{1}{n_g-1} \big(1-\frac{w_g}{\sum_{h=1}^G w_h}\big)^2\Big]^{-1}\). For \(G = 2\) it is Welch’s \(t\)-test (\(F^* = T^2\) and \(\Delta = df\)).

from statsmodels.stats.oneway import anova_oneway
anova_oneway(data = ..., groups = ..., use_var = "unequal", welch_correction = True)
  • Needs approximately normal data without outliers (for small or moderate \(n_g\)).
  • Does not require equal variances. If they happen to be equal it loses only a little power compared to Fisher’s ANOVA: our default choice.

Kruskal-Wallis test

Model for \(G\) groups, without normality: \(X_{i(g)} = {\color{#eb078e}{\theta_{g}}} + \varepsilon_{i(g)}\), \(\varepsilon_{i(g)} \overset{iid}{\sim} (0,\sigma^{2})\), where \(\theta_g\) is the population location of group \(g\).

\[H_0: {\color{#6A5ACD}{\theta_1}} {\color{#eb078e}{=}} {\color{#06bcf1}{\theta_2}} {\color{#eb078e}{=}} \ldots {\color{#eb078e}{=}} {\color{#8bb174}{\theta_G}} \quad \text{vs} \quad H_a: \theta_i {\color{#eb078e}{\neq}} \theta_j \ \text{ for at least one pair } (i,j).\]

The test statistic applies the ANOVA idea to the ranks of the pooled sample, not to the values: it measures how far the mean rank of each group, \(\overline{R}_g\), is from the mean of all ranks, \((N+1)/2\):

\[H = \frac{12}{N(N+1)} \sum_{g=1}^G n_g \left(\overline{R}_g - \frac{N+1}{2}\right)^2 \ {\overset{H_0}{\sim}} \ \chi^2(G-1).\]

With ties, \(H\) is divided by \(1 - \sum_{v=1}^V (t_v^3 - t_v)/(N^3 - N)\), where \(t_v\) is the number of observations sharing the \(v\)-th distinct value.

from scipy import stats
stats.kruskal(group_1, group_2, group_3)
  • Robust: an extreme value only changes its rank, however large it is.
  • To compare means (\(\theta_g = \mu_g\)) the distributions should be symmetric with equal variances; otherwise it compares locations (medians). Slightly less powerful than the ANOVAs when their assumptions hold.
